# WildfireGuardian evaluation: demonstration report

seed `20260919` | wg-eval 0.1.0

This report is generated by `experiments/run_demonstration.py`. It analyses
synthetic records whose true effects are fixed by the generator, so every
number below can be checked against a truth the analysis cannot see.

It is a demonstration of *analysis failure modes*, not of any system's
performance. No real experiment output is involved.

Read it as four paired examples. In each, the left-hand analysis is one a
competent person could plausibly run, and the right-hand analysis is the one
this library performs by default.


## 1. The pseudoreplication error

**Scenario** `resident_bootstrap_false_precision` — Resident-level bootstrap manufactures precision

- **Trap**: Resampling residents treats 1,200 nested observations as 1,200 experiments and returns a confidence interval several times too narrow.
- **Truth**: The design supplies 20 independent worlds. The true mean loss difference is +0.6.
- **Defence**: Resample whole worlds; residents travel with the world they belong to.
- **Records**: `resident_bootstrap_false_precision.parquet` (2400 rows, `sha256:010a21f565fad7370435a9008d5118523629b26e9bfa3a5fa430fbdb56fdced1`)

```
records: 2400 observations in 20 worlds (60 observations per world per policy)
true mean loss difference (B - A): +0.600

  WRONG  resident-level bootstrap: +1.161 [+0.902, +1.421]  width 0.519
  RIGHT  world-level paired bootstrap: +1.161 [+0.192, +2.205]  width 2.013

  width ratio (right / wrong): 3.9x
  measured coverage over 60 replications -- resident: 62%, world: 90% (nominal 95%)
  design effect for policy_a: 1.9 (ICC 0.88, 40 observations worth 21 independent ones)
```

- **resident-level resampling understates uncertainty** — The correct interval is 3.9x wider. The naive analysis claims 1200 independent units where the design supplies 20.
- **the understatement shows up as under-coverage** — Over 60 independent replications of the whole experiment, the nominal-95% resident-level interval covered the true difference 62% of the time; the world-level interval covered it 90% of the time.

Trap reproduced: **True** · Defence held: **True**

## 2. The correct paired, world-level analysis

**Scenario** `world_bootstrap_correct` — World-level bootstrap is calibrated

- **Trap**: A single interval proves nothing; calibration has to be measured over repeated experiments.
- **Truth**: The true mean loss difference is +0.6 in every replication.
- **Defence**: Measure coverage: a nominal 95% interval should contain the truth about 95% of the time.
- **Records**: `world_bootstrap_correct.parquet` (2400 rows, `sha256:010a21f565fad7370435a9008d5118523629b26e9bfa3a5fa430fbdb56fdced1`)

```
true mean loss difference (B - A): +0.600
  world-level paired interval on this draw: [+0.192, +2.205] -> covers the truth
  measured coverage over 100 replications at 20 worlds: 90% (nominal 95%)
  measured coverage over 50 replications at 60 worlds: 98% (nominal 95%)
  for contrast, resident-level coverage: 61%
  mean interval width -- world: 1.723, resident: 0.659
```

- **world-level cluster bootstrap is approximately calibrated** — Across 100 replications the nominal-95% world-level interval covered the true difference 90% of the time at 20 worlds and 98% at 60 worlds. The percentile bootstrap under-covers slightly when clusters are few, which is why the validator warns below 20 clusters rather than staying silent -- but it is calibrated in a way the resident-level interval never becomes, at any number of residents.
- **more residents per world cannot fix a resident-level interval** — The resident-level interval narrows as residents are added while its coverage stays broken, because the missing variance is between worlds. Only more worlds buy precision that is real.

Trap reproduced: **True** · Defence held: **True**

## 3. Equivalence testing against a declared margin

**Scenario** `practical_equivalence` — Practically equivalent, and provably so

- **Trap**: The interval includes zero, so the report says 'no significant difference' and readers hear 'the same'.
- **Truth**: The true difference is +0.03 against a practical margin of 0.40 -- real, and negligible.
- **Defence**: TOST against the declared margin turns 'we failed to detect' into 'we established equivalence'.
- **Records**: `practical_equivalence.parquet` (24000 rows, `sha256:94e2b38f3181dc9efe1ec2e4908ab0ec7dc31786867fd8f75884fea4e5f10e16`)

```
  true difference: +0.030, declared practical margin: +/-0.4
  paired difference: -0.0055 [-0.1068, +0.0970]  (bootstrap p = 0.885)

  WRONG  'p > 0.05, therefore the policies are the same.'
  RIGHT  Equivalent within +/-0.4: the 90% interval for the difference (-0.09097, 0.08149) lies entirely inside the declared practical margin.
  verdict: equivalent -- mean_loss: policy_b and policy_a are practically equivalent within +/-0.4. The interval (-0.09097, 0.08149) fits inside the margin.
```

- **equivalence requires a margin, and with one it is concludable** — Equivalent within +/-0.4: the 90% interval for the difference (-0.09097, 0.08149) lies entirely inside the declared practical margin.

Trap reproduced: **True** · Defence held: **True**

## 4. Tail-risk disagreement

**Scenario** `tail_risk_disagreement` — Mean favours A while tail risk favours B

- **Trap**: policy_a has the lower average loss, so a mean-only report recommends it.
- **Truth**: policy_a carries a 4% per-observation chance of a +60 loss; its 90% CVaR is far worse than policy_b's while its mean is 0.8 better.
- **Defence**: Declare tail metrics up front and report the disagreement rather than resolving it silently.
- **Records**: `tail_risk_disagreement.parquet` (7200 rows, `sha256:f23f0cb04377cc16f9d18cdf4eb05e905e550a8d23cc7f6d8e8146d536006160`)

```
  mean_loss   B - A = +0.846 [+0.365, +1.332] -> favours policy_a
  cvar90_loss B - A = -2.709 [-4.710, -1.216] -> favours policy_b

  TAIL DISAGREEMENT on `loss`: mean_loss favours policy_a while cvar90_loss favours policy_b. Average performance and tail risk point in opposite directions; reporting either alone would mislead.
  METRIC SPLIT for policy_b vs policy_a: different declared metrics resolve in favour of different policies (mean_loss->policy_a, cvar90_loss->policy_b, success_rate->policy_a). There is no single winner; the choice depends on which metric the decision actually cares about, and that must be declared in advance.
```

- **mean and tail resolve in opposite directions** — Reporting either statistic alone yields a confident and opposite recommendation. The disagreement is the result, and the decision needs a pre-declared risk preference to resolve it.

Trap reproduced: **True** · Defence held: **True**

## What the four demonstrations establish

| # | Failure mode | What produces it | What prevents it |
|---|--------------|------------------|------------------|
| 1 | False precision | Resampling nested observations as if they were independent experiments | Resample whole worlds; observations travel with their world |
| 2 | Unverified calibration | Trusting one interval because it looks reasonable | Measure coverage over repeated replications of the whole experiment |
| 3 | "No significant difference" read as "the same" | A large p-value and no declared margin | TOST against an explicit practical margin, or say "undetermined" |
| 4 | A confident recommendation from one statistic | Reporting the mean of a heavy-tailed outcome | Declare tail metrics in advance and report the disagreement |

Two further failure modes are exercised by the full red-team suite in
`redteam.md`: a ranking reversed by missing worlds, and a policy that wins only
because it was allocated easier worlds.

## The rules these demonstrations encode

1. The unit of inference is the world. Residents are nested observations, and
   no number of them buys precision that generalises to new fires.
2. Policies evaluated on the same world are compared within that world. Anything
   else confounds the policy with the material it was given.
3. An interval that includes zero means **undetermined**. Only a test against a
   declared practical margin can support equivalence.
4. Where metrics disagree, the disagreement is the finding.
